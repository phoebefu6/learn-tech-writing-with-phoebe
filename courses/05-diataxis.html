Diátaxis: the four kinds of docs

"The documentation" is not one thing. It is four different jobs wearing one name, and mixing them is why doc sets feel like they're failing even when every fact in them is true. Today you learn the map, the compass, and you write Pulse's first real how-to guide.

Part 0

Why this session exists

You have felt it: the README that teaches when you need steps, the wiki page that lists options when you need to understand why. One framework explains all of it. Diátaxis, created by Daniele Procida (free at diataxis.fr), says users need four distinct kinds of documentation - tutorial, how-to guide, reference, explanation - and that most doc pain is one kind doing another kind's job. Pulse already has an audience statement, a plan, and a README; today we find the gap the framework exposes.

Part 1 · covers diataxis.fr "Start here" + "Tutorials" + "How-to guides"

The map 8 min live

Two questions generate the whole framework. Is the user doing something practical (action) or acquiring theory (cognition)? And are they at study, building skill (acquisition), or at work, applying it (application)? Two axes, four quadrants, four kinds of docs. Each quadrant answers a different question a user actually asks.

LiveTwo axes, four quadrants3 min▶

Each kind of documentation answers one canonical user question, and refuses the other three:

  • Tutorial - "Can you teach me to...?" A lesson. The user is at study, doing practical work, and the teacher is responsible for their success: visible results at every step, one safe path, no alternatives, minimal explanation, absolute reliability.
  • How-to guide - "How do I...?" Directions for a goal. The user is at work, already competent, and needs steps that adapt to their real, messy situation.
  • Reference - "What is...?" Description. Dry, list-like, consulted while working. It mirrors the structure of the product itself, like a map mirrors territory.
  • Explanation - "Why...?" Discussion. Read away from the keyboard, when the user steps back to understand context, design decisions, and trade-offs.
★ Try it nowTake the last doc you opened at work and ask the two compass questions: 1. Does it inform ACTION (steps to take) or COGNITION (things to know)? 2. Does it serve ACQUISITION (someone learning) or APPLICATION (someone working)? Two answers = one quadrant. If you got different answers for different sections of the same page, congratulations: you found a fused doc.
LiveTutorial vs how-to: the most-conflated pair3 min▶

Both are practical and step-shaped, which is why merging them is the single most common mistake in software docs. But they serve opposite situations:

TutorialHow-to guide
User isat study, building skillat work, mid-task
Settingcontrived and safe, chosen by youtheir real, messy environment
Pathone fixed linear path, no alternativesbranches for real-world variation
Responsibilitythe teacher owns the learner's successthe user owns the outcome
Analogya cooking lesson with an instructora recipe in your own kitchen

The tell: a "tutorial" that opens with "depending on your setup, you may want to..." is a how-to guide in costume. A "how-to" that pauses to explain what a virtual environment is has a tutorial trapped inside it.

Real world

The getting-started page that served no one. A data platform's "Getting started" mixed a toy walkthrough with production deployment options. Newcomers hit the branching options and froze; experienced engineers waded through the toy example to find the one flag they needed. Split into a tutorial (fixed sample data, guaranteed outcome) and a deployment how-to, both audiences got faster - the content barely changed, only its sorting did.

Self-studyReference vs explanation: the litmus test2 min read▶

The cognition side has its own commonly-fused pair. The litmus test is when the reader has it open:

  • Reference is consulted WHILE working. It is austere on purpose: tables, parameter lists, one true format. It states facts and cites nothing but the product. Its structure should mirror the product's structure, so the user can navigate the doc by navigating their mental model of the code.
  • Explanation is read when stepping back. It is discursive and wide-angle: why the design is this way, what the alternatives were, how this connects to that. It is the one quadrant where opinion and perspective are welcome.

If a "reference" page starts arguing for a design decision, cut the argument and move it to an explanation page. If an "architecture overview" keeps getting opened mid-incident for one config value, that value belongs in reference. Session 6 builds both kinds for Pulse.

Part 2 · covers diataxis.fr "The compass" + "How to use Diátaxis"

The compass and the workflow 7 min live

The map tells you what the four kinds are. The compass tells you where you are standing right now, with a real messy page in front of you. And the workflow rule tells you what to do about it - which is emphatically NOT what most teams do first.

LiveThe compass on messy real pages3 min▶

Real pages rarely arrive pre-labeled. The compass is the two questions from the map, applied to whatever is in front of you: action or cognition? then acquisition or application? Ask them about each chunk of a page, not the page as a whole.

Worked example: "Is our wiki page on the alert system a how-to or a reference?" Run the compass section by section and the honest answer is: it's three docs fused. The threshold table (cognition + application) is reference. The what-to-do-when-it-fires steps (action + application) are a how-to. The paragraph on why we alert on volume instead of latency (cognition + acquisition) is explanation. The page isn't bad because the facts are wrong; it's bad because three different readers each have to wade through the other two readers' content.

Writing tip The compass works before writing too. About to type? Ask the two questions about what the READER will be doing, name the quadrant, and let that quadrant's rules settle your tone, structure, and what to leave out.
LiveThe iteration rule: never restructure top-down3 min▶

The classic failure mode after learning Diátaxis: declare a grand migration, create four empty sections labeled Tutorials, How-to, Reference, Explanation, and start shoveling the old wiki into them. The diataxis.fr site itself is blunt about this: don't do that, it's horrible. You burn weeks, the empty skeleton demoralizes everyone, and the fused pages stay fused - just in new folders.

The prescribed workflow is deliberately small:

  • Pick any small piece of the existing docs. Literally any page, wherever you happen to be.
  • Assess it with the compass. What is it trying to be? What is it fused with? What single thing would most improve it?
  • Make ONE improvement and publish it. Move the reference table out. Sharpen one title. Delete one digression.
  • Repeat. Structure emerges from many small correct moves, the way a city street grid emerges, not the way a planned monument does.

The mindset shift underneath: documentation is never "finished", and under this workflow it is always complete - every published state is a valid, shippable state. There is no half-migrated limbo.

Self-studyComplex hierarchies: audience first, then the four needs2 min read▶

Big products break the naive four-folder layout: a platform serving analysts, engineers, and admins can't share one tutorial section. The guidance for scale:

  • Segment by user type or context FIRST, then apply the four needs within each segment. "Analyst docs" gets its own tutorial, how-tos, reference; so does "operator docs". The audience statement from Session 1 is exactly this segmentation, already written.
  • Cap contents lists at roughly seven items. Past that, readers stop scanning and start searching, which means your structure has stopped helping. Split or regroup.
  • Make landing pages readable overviews, not bare link farms. A good landing page tells the reader which segment they are and which need they have, in prose, before showing them the list.

Pulse now has four prose artifacts: docs/AUDIENCE.md, docs/PLAN.md, docs/notes-rewrite.md, and the README from Session 4. Run the compass over each and find what the set is missing.

Compass the README: the quickstart with expected outputs is action + application (how-to), the config table is cognition + application (reference). Verdict: a fusion - and that's fine. ★ The audit's lesson The compass didn't tell us to restructure anything. It classified what exists, blessed the README's fusion as appropriate for a front door, and pointed at one missing doc for one named audience. That is the framework working as intended: one small next move, not a migration.
Build-along 2 of 3

Write the how-to: diagnose a LOW day ★ 10 min · build your own

Time to fill the gap. A how-to guide is recipe-shaped: a precisely-named goal, prerequisites, numbered actionable steps, zero teaching, zero digression. Where the reader might need background, we link to reference instead of explaining inline.

Create docs/how-to-diagnose-low-day.md. Note the title names the exact task in the user's own words - a how-to's title is its search result.

Copy the guide below. Read it as the 2am engineer: every step is an action with a decision at the end, and nothing explains how the pipeline works.

Check it against the how-to rules: prerequisites stated, steps adapt to the real world (the branches live inside steps 2-4), no teaching, ends when the goal is reached.

docs/how-to-diagnose-low-day.md# How to diagnose a LOW day in Pulse Use this guide when a row in daily_metrics.csv shows LOW in the alert column (events below alerts.min_daily_events in config.yaml, default 50). Prerequisites: a checkout of pulse/ and the run that produced the alert. 1. Open daily_metrics.csv and find the LOW row. Note the day and its events count. 2. Check the cleaned data volume for that day. If cleaned rows are near zero, the loss happened before metrics.py - continue to step 3. 3. Check that the raw files for that day actually arrived in pulse/data/raw. Missing or empty files mean an upstream delivery failure: stop here, that is your cause. 4. Check for a spike in filtered rows: clean.py drops events whose type is not in config.yaml valid_types (page_view, signup, purchase, api_call). A sudden filter spike usually means an upstream schema or naming change. 5. Decide: raw files missing or filter spiked = data problem. Raw volume genuinely low with normal filtering = real traffic drop. 6. Escalate with evidence: the day, the events count, and which step (3, 4, or 5) localized the cause. For column and config key definitions, see the config reference (Session 6). This guide does not explain how the pipeline works.
Writing tip Notice what restraint bought: the guide never defines "cleaned data" or teaches YAML, because its reader is competent and mid-incident. The two sentences at the bottom do the linking-not-teaching move - the how-to points at reference and refuses to become explanation.
Build-along 3 of 3

Quadrant-sort your own team wiki ★ 6 min · build your own

The compass only becomes yours when you run it on pages you actually own. This exercise takes five pages and produces a number you can report: how many of your docs are fused.

Pick five pages from your team's wiki or docs folder - the five most-visited if you know them, otherwise the five you touched most recently.

Compass each one section by section: action or cognition, study or work. Give each page a verdict: one clean quadrant, or fused (name the quadrants inside it).

Count the fused ones. Three or more out of five is typical, so don't be discouraged - be specific. For ONE fused page, name the single improvement you'd publish this week, per the iteration rule.

Do not create four empty folders. Not even a little bit.

Real world

The one-page-a-week migration. A five-person data team scored 9 of their 12 wiki pages as fused, then fixed exactly one page per week: usually by moving a reference table out of a how-to. After a quarter, without ever announcing a restructure, their docs had quietly become navigable - and the habit survived because no single week's work exceeded an hour.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · An engineer mid-incident needs the exact meaning of the alert column in daily_metrics.csv. Which doc serves them?

Compass it: they need to KNOW something (cognition), and they are at WORK (application). That quadrant is reference. The other two would make a 2am incident longer.

2 · What actually separates a tutorial from a how-to guide?

The difference is the user's situation, not length or skill flattery: contrived-safe vs real-world, single path vs branching, teacher-responsible vs user-responsible. It's the most-conflated pair for a reason.

3 · Your team just learned Diátaxis and wants to fix the wiki. What does the framework say to do first?

The top-down empty-skeleton restructure is the failure mode the site warns against by name. Structure emerges from small published improvements; docs are always complete, never finished.
