Accessible writing

Part 1 · covers Google's accessibility course "inclusive design"

Design for everyone 10 min read

Accessible writing is not a bolt-on for a small minority of readers. It is the same craft this whole course teaches - plain language, clear structure, honest labels - applied so that it holds up for every reader, on every device, in every condition.

Self-studyThe curb-cut effect4 min read▶

Curb cuts - the little ramps where a sidewalk meets the street - were fought for by wheelchair users. Then everyone else started using them: parents with strollers, travelers with suitcases, delivery workers with carts. Accessibility features consistently follow this pattern: built for a specific need, useful to everyone.

  • High-contrast text serves low-vision readers, and everyone reading a laptop in sunlight.
  • Text-to-speech and captions serve blind and Deaf users, and everyone listening to a doc on a commute or watching a video in a quiet office.
  • Keyboard access serves people who cannot use a mouse, and every power user who never wanted to.
  • Plain language serves readers with cognitive disabilities, and every exhausted engineer skimming at 2am - Session 1's audience made universal.
★ The reframe You never have to choose between "accessible" and "good". Every rule in this deep dive makes your docs measurably better for your entire audience. Accessibility is quality with the excuses removed.
Self-studyPermanent, temporary, situational3 min read▶

Google's accessibility course teaches disability as a mismatch between a person and a design, not a fixed attribute of a person. The same barrier hits three kinds of readers:

KindExampleHits the same barrier
PermanentA reader who is blindAn unlabeled chart carries zero information
TemporaryEye surgery recovery, reading via screen reader for a monthSame chart, same zero
SituationalDriving, listening to a doc read aloudSame chart, same zero

Design for the permanent case and the temporary and situational cases come along for free. That multiplies your audience: the number of people who will ever experience your doc through a screen reader, a zoomed screen, or a monochrome e-reader is far larger than any disability statistic suggests.

Part 2 · the working rules, consolidated

The five rule groups 30 min read

Every accessibility guideline you will meet in a doc review falls into one of five groups: alt text, contrast, inclusive language, accessible visuals, and structural editing. Learn the five and you can audit any doc without a checklist in front of you.

Self-studyRule group 1 · Alt text6 min read▶

Alt text is what a screen reader speaks in place of an image. The rules, agreed by every source:

  • Describe the image in context. The same pipeline diagram gets different alt text in a quickstart ("the three Pulse scripts run in order") than in an architecture doc ("ingest writes raw CSVs that clean filters before metrics aggregates").
  • Keep it short: a phrase or one to two sentences. Alt text is spoken linearly; a paragraph traps the listener.
  • Decorative images get alt="" - empty, not missing. Empty tells the screen reader to skip; missing makes it read the filename aloud.
  • Complex images split: a short alt ("daily event counts for July, described below") plus a full explanation in body text, where every reader benefits from it.
  • Never start with "Image of..." - the screen reader already announced it is an image. And no all-caps: some screen readers spell capitalized words letter by letter.
Weak alt textalt="IMAGE OF CHART"
Working alt textalt="Daily event counts for July. July 12 drops to 31 events, below the 50-event alert threshold."
Self-studyRule group 2 · Contrast, with numbers4 min read▶

Contrast is the one rule group with hard numbers, so use them:

  • 4.5:1 minimum contrast ratio for small text against its background.
  • 3:1 minimum for large text (roughly 18pt and up, or 14pt bold).
  • Verify with a tool - the WebAIM Contrast Checker takes two hex codes and gives a pass or fail. Never eyeball it: your calibrated monitor in a dim office is the best-case scenario, not the median reader's.
Writing tip The most common failure in data-team docs is not body text - it is chart annotations and light-grey "subtle" captions. Subtle is a synonym for invisible at 4.5:1. Check the greys first.
Self-studyRule group 3 · Inclusive language6 min read▶

Language about people carries assumptions, and docs travel further than you plan. The consolidated guidance:

  • Center the person, not the condition: "a person who is blind", "people with limited mobility". Avoid framing disability as deficiency: no "normal users" versus everyone else, no "healthy" as the contrast to disabled.
  • Drop the pity verbs: not "victim of", "suffers from", or "wheelchair-bound" - a wheelchair is mobility, not confinement. "Uses a wheelchair" states the fact.
  • Respect identity-first preferences. Many Deaf and neurodivergent people prefer identity-first language ("Deaf person", "autistic person"). When you write for or about a specific community, their preference wins over the general rule.
  • Microsoft's bias-free additions: gender-neutral language ("they", "chairperson", example names drawn from many cultures), no militaristic metaphors ("kill the process" becomes "stop the process" where it is not a literal command name), and diverse example names instead of ten Johns.
Self-studyRule group 4 · Accessible visuals4 min read▶

The visual rule that catches the most data-team artifacts: never rely on color, pattern, or position ALONE to carry meaning. Red-means-bad fails for colorblind readers, printed pages, and screen readers all at once.

  • Pair every color with a text label. A red cell that also says FAIL survives every rendering; a red cell alone survives none of them.
  • Every visual gets alt text - charts, screenshots, diagrams, the lot. Rule group 1 applies to all of them.
Real world

Pulse got this right by accident. Pulse's daily_metrics.csv flags a bad day with the word LOW in an alert column - a text flag, not a red cell. That is accidentally perfect accessibility: it survives a screen reader, a monochrome printout, grep, and a colorblind on-call engineer. If the alert had been a colored cell in a spreadsheet, it would fail all four. When you design a status signal, make it a word first and a color second.

Self-studyRule group 5 · The structural editing checklist6 min read▶

The last group is how the doc is built, not what it says:

  • Real heading elements, no skipped levels. Screen-reader users navigate by heading; a bolded line styled to look like a heading is invisible to them, and jumping from H1 to H4 scrambles the outline.
  • Descriptive link text. Screen readers can list every link on a page out of context - a list of nine "click here"s is useless. "See the config reference" works everywhere.
  • Plain language, active voice, short sentences. Session 2's rules are also accessibility rules - cognitive load is an access barrier.
  • Convert dense paragraphs to lists when they carry parallel items - exactly Session 2's paragraphs-to-lists move.
  • Test it for real: zoom to 200%, navigate with keyboard only, then listen to the page with the screen reader already on your machine (VoiceOver on a Mac, Narrator on Windows). Ten minutes of listening teaches more than an hour of guidelines.
Build-along

Audit Pulse's docs ★ 35 min · build your own

You have written a README, a how-to, a reference, and an ADR for Pulse across the sessions. Now run the five rule groups over them as a real audit - the same pass you would run on any doc set you inherit.

Open README.md and docs/how-to-diagnose-low-day.md. Check the heading outline first: are they real markdown headings, and does any level get skipped (an # followed by an ###)? Fix the outline before anything else - it is the skeleton everything hangs on.

Hunt link text. Search both files for "here" and "this link". Rewrite each hit to say where it goes: "see the config reference", "the ADR explains the UTC decision".

Hunt color-only meaning. Pulse's LOW flag passes (it is text), but check any chart or screenshot you added along the way: does anything communicate by color alone? Add the text label.

Check every image for alt text. Decorative ones get alt=""; informative ones get the takeaway in one sentence, per rule group 1.

Search for "simply" and "just". Session 1 called them curse-of-knowledge alarms; they are also accessibility problems - they tell a struggling reader the failure is theirs. Delete them.

Fix your top findings, then add one line to DOCS.md so the standard outlives the audit.

Add to DOCS.md norms## Accessibility Every doc: real heading levels (no skips), descriptive link text (never "click here"), alt text on every image (alt="" if decorative), no color-only meaning, contrast checked at 4.5:1 for text in any visual. Audit with the five rule groups before publishing.
★ What you just built An audit you can run on any doc set in under an hour, and a one-paragraph norm that makes the next doc accessible by default instead of by retrofit. The rule groups are the reusable asset; Pulse was just the practice ground.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · The minimum contrast ratio for small body text is...

4.5:1 for small text, 3:1 only for large text - and always tool-verified. Your monitor in your lighting is the best case, not the median reader's.

2 · A purely decorative divider image should get...

Empty alt says "skip me". Missing alt makes many screen readers announce the filename, and describing decoration wastes the listener's time.

3 · Pulse flags a bad day with the word LOW in a column instead of a red cell. Why does that matter for accessibility?

The rule is never color ALONE. Color plus a text label is fine; the LOW flag shows the text half doing all the real work.
